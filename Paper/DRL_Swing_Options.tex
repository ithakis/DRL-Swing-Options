\begin{abstract}
    We study the valuation of electricity swing options under spot dynamics with seasonality, mean reversion, and spikes. Prices follow the Hambly--Howison--Kluge (HHK) model, and the contract is cast as a finite-horizon stochastic control problem with continuous exercise quantities, a cumulative volume cap, and convex exercise costs. We propose a deterministic actor--critic method whose profitability-gated actor clips candidate exercises to quantities with non-negative immediate payoff while preserving gradient flow via a straight-through estimator. The core method, denoted AC-sample, is \emph{model-agnostic} in the spot dynamics: it learns from simulated price paths and uses a single-sample temporal-difference bootstrap, so it requires no transition density. We also study AC-kernel, an HHK-specific extension that replaces the sampled bootstrap with a semi-analytical expected target when the transition law is known. Both variants avoid explicit state-space discretisation and operate on a nine-dimensional engineered feature vector that combines market factors with contract bookkeeping variables. Numerical experiments compare the actor--critic variants with a full-state discretized-action least-squares Monte Carlo baseline (LSM-D) across a grid of convex-cost configurations.\chg{abstract results reframed around the DP reference} \hlt{Every comparison is anchored to a near-exact grid dynamic-programming reference that the two-factor structure makes feasible (per-cell numerical uncertainty at or below \mbox{$2\times10^{-4}$}, verified by convergence and forward-simulation studies). The reference certifies the absolute distance of each method from the optimum: the model-agnostic AC-sample trails it by \mbox{$0.9$--$3.1\%$}, the kernel extension AC-kernel is uniformly within \mbox{$0.3\%$} across the entire grid---with seed-paired confidence intervals roughly an order of magnitude tighter than the model-agnostic variant---and the coarse \mbox{$M{=}5$} LSM-D baseline falls up to \mbox{$5.1\%$} short under high convexity, an action-grid discretisation error that a finer grid mostly removes.} Beyond valuation, a focal risk study computes common-random-number finite-difference Greeks and daily-rebalanced delta hedges.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Electricity markets display several features that distinguish them from traditional financial markets: non-storability, strong seasonality, abrupt demand--supply imbalances, and short-lived price spikes. A substantial literature therefore models electricity prices using mean-reverting diffusions augmented by jump components. Early two-factor Gaussian models were introduced by~\cite{lucia2002electricity}, while~\cite{cartea2005pricing} incorporated jumps to capture extreme movements. A major refinement was provided by~\cite{Hambly2009}, who specified the log spot price as the sum of a seasonal component, an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck factor, and a rapidly mean-reverting spike factor. The resulting model captures both ordinary price fluctuations and transient spikes while remaining tractable enough for calibration and valuation. Swing options are central in gas and power markets because they grant the holder repeated rights to purchase energy subject to volume and operational constraints. Their valuation is substantially more involved than that of European-style derivatives, since the holder repeatedly decides how much to exercise at each decision time. Classical approaches include tree and grid-based dynamic programming~\cite{jaillet2004valuation}, Monte Carlo regression methods~\cite{longstaff2001valuing,meinshausen2004monte}, dual or multiple-stopping formulations~\cite{carmona2008swing}, and analytically tractable special cases. These methods can be accurate, but they become increasingly cumbersome when the underlying model contains multiple stochastic factors, jump components, and non-trivial contractual constraints. Reinforcement learning and neuro-dynamic programming provide an attractive alternative to explicit state-space discretisation~\cite{bertsekas1996neuro,sutton1998reinforcement}. Recent financial applications include deep hedging, optimal stopping, and related control problems~\cite{halperin2017qlbs,buehler2019deephedging,becker2019deepoptimalstopping,tsoskounoglou2025higherorder}. Closely related work already applies least-squares Monte Carlo and reinforcement-learning algorithms to commodity swing options~\cite{daluiso2020pricingcommodityswing}, and deep Q-learning has been used for multiple-stopping and swing-style examples with primal--dual bounds~\cite{ery2021solvingoptimalstopping}. Against this background, our contribution is an engineering adaptation of continuous-action actor--critic learning to a convex-cost swing testbed, together with an explicit comparison between a model-agnostic sampled target and a model-specific HHK expected target\hlt{---both measured against a near-exact grid dynamic-programming reference that the two-factor structure of the testbed makes feasible}.\chg{new DP-reference clause}

We formulate swing-option valuation under the HHK model as a finite-horizon Markov decision process and solve the resulting control problem with deterministic actor--critic methods. The headline method, AC-sample, is \emph{model-agnostic} in the spot dynamics: it learns from simulated price paths and never requires the transition density, so the same algorithm applies to any energy price model one can simulate. The HHK model also admits a tractable transition law; we therefore report AC-kernel as a model-specific extension that trades generality for a lower-variance critic target. The focus is on contracts with optional exercise, a cumulative volume cap, and convex exercise costs, since convexity destroys the bang-bang structure that simplifies many classical implementations. Our main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a \textbf{profitability-gated actor} that clips proposed exercises to quantities with non-negative immediate payoff while preserving gradient flow through a straight-through estimator;
    \item a \textbf{nine-dimensional feature representation} that combines HHK market factors with contract bookkeeping variables in an engineered Markovian input to the policy and value networks;
    \item a \textbf{two-variant actor--critic comparison}: AC-sample uses a model-agnostic single-sample bootstrap, while AC-kernel uses the semi-analytical HHK transition kernel when that model structure is available;
    \item \chg{new contribution item: DP reference}\hlt{a \textbf{near-exact dynamic-programming reference} for the two-factor HHK testbed---a grid Bellman solver with analytically quadratured transitions and per-cell verified numerical-uncertainty bands---that certifies the absolute distance of every method from the optimum};
    \item a set of \textbf{numerical experiments} comparing both actor--critic variants with a full-state discretized-action LSM baseline (LSM-D) across convex cost specifications\hlt{, all anchored to the DP reference}; and
    \item a \textbf{risk-management study} that computes common-random-number finite-difference Greeks and runs daily-rebalanced delta hedges in a focal regime.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:market_contract} introduces the HHK price model and the swing-contract specification used throughout the manuscript. Section~\ref{sec:classical_methods} summarises the classical dynamic-programming perspective that motivates the reinforcement-learning approach. Section~\ref{sec:rl_formulation} presents the Markov decision process, actor--critic architecture, and profitability gate. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports numerical results, \hlt{anchored to the near-exact DP reference of Section}~\ref{sec:dp_reference}; Section~\ref{sec:hedging} studies sensitivities and hedging, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Market and Contract Framework}
\label{sec:market_contract}

\subsection{Electricity Spot Price Dynamics: the HHK Model}

We model the electricity spot price directly under a pricing measure $\mathbb{Q}$ calibrated to observed forward prices. This is standard in reduced-form energy modelling: rather than deriving a unique martingale measure from storage-based no-arbitrage arguments, one specifies a tractable pricing dynamics consistent with traded forward curves; see~\cite{lucia2002electricity,cartea2005pricing,Hambly2009}. Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0},\mathbb{Q})$ be a filtered probability space supporting a Brownian motion $W$ and an independent Poisson process $N$ with intensity $\lambda$.

Following~\cite{Hambly2009}, the spot price is written as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:model}
    S_t = \exp\!\bigl(f(t) + X_t + Y_t\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $f$ is a deterministic seasonal function and the latent factors satisfy
\begin{align}
    \D X_t & = -\alpha X_t \,\D t + \sigma \,\D W_t, \label{eq:OU} \\
    \D Y_t & = -\beta Y_t \,\D t + J_t \,\D N_t. \label{eq:jumpOU}
\end{align}
Here $\alpha > 0$ is the mean-reversion rate of the diffusive factor $X$, $\beta > 0$ is the mean-reversion rate of the spike factor $Y$, and typically $\beta \gg \alpha$ so that spikes decay quickly. The jump sizes $(J_t)$ are i.i.d.\ and non-negative, taken to be exponentially distributed with mean $\mu_J > 0$, following~\cite{Hambly2009,cartea2005pricing}. Restricting jumps to be positive models the empirical observation that electricity price spikes are predominantly upward.

The deterministic term $f(t)$ captures predictable seasonality in demand and supply. A simple illustration is
\[
    f(t) = \log(S_0) + A \cos(2 \pi t),
\]
although in practice one can use richer parametrisations. In the numerical experiments of Section~\ref{sec:experiments} we set $A = 0$, so that $f(t) = \log S_0$ and the seasonal component is flat. Under~\eqref{eq:model}--\eqref{eq:jumpOU}, the pair $(X_t,Y_t)$ is Markov and the distributional quantities needed for forward-curve fitting can be expressed semi-analytically; see~\cite{Hambly2009}. In particular, one may recover the seasonal component so that the model matches observed forwards via
\[
    F_t^{[T]} = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[S_T \mid \mathcal{F}_t].
\]
To ensure forward-curve consistency, $f$ must absorb a convexity correction arising from the variance of $X_T$ and the moment-generating function of $Y_T$ under $\mathbb{Q}$; details are given in~\cite{Hambly2009}.

The choice $A = 0$ is motivated by the short contract maturity of approximately one month used in the numerical experiments.
Over a horizon of $T \approx 0.083$ years ($\approx 22$ trading days), the deterministic seasonal component $A\cos(2\pi t)$ changes by at most $|A|\cdot 2\pi T \approx 0.5A$, which is negligible relative to the diffusive and jump volatility for realistic values of $A$.
Setting $A = 0$ therefore isolates the contribution of the stochastic factors $(X,Y)$ without confounding seasonal trends, and keeps the pricing problem well-suited to comparing RL with LSM-D on a well-understood benchmark.
For longer-maturity contracts or seasonal calibration studies, $A > 0$ can be activated without modifying the algorithm.
We emphasise that the numerical experiments use this $f(t)=\log S_0$ specification as a \emph{normalised testbed} rather than a forward-calibrated model: with $f$ flat, the implied forward curve $F_0^{[T]}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[S_T]$ is the (mildly upward-sloping) curve generated by the $(X,Y)$ dynamics through the convexity correction above, not a separately fitted flat curve. Calibrating to an observed forward curve would absorb that correction into $f$ and could change absolute price levels and payoff distributions; it would not affect the methodological comparison here, since AC-sample, AC-kernel, and LSM-D are all evaluated under the same simulated dynamics and the same pricing measure.

The HHK model provides an appealing compromise between realism and tractability. It captures the empirical features that matter most for electricity options, yet it already makes classical grid-based valuation demanding because the state space contains two latent factors and a jump component. This is precisely the regime in which simulation-based control methods become attractive.

\subsection{Swing Contract Specification}

We consider a finite set of $n$ exercise dates
\[
    0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_{n-1} = T,
    \qquad
    t_i = i \Delta t,
\]
with a constant time step $\Delta t = T/(n-1)$, so that the $n$ decision dates span $n-1$ intervals and the final exercise opportunity coincides with maturity. At each exercise date $t_i$, the holder chooses a non-negative quantity
\[
    q_i \in [0,q_{\max}],
\]
where $q_{\max}$ is the maximum offtake per exercise date. The cumulative exercised volume is tracked by
\[
    Q_0 = 0,
    \qquad
    Q_{i+1} = Q_i + q_i,
\]
and the contract imposes the cumulative cap
\[
    Q_n = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} q_i \le Q_{\max}.
\]
Throughout the reported experiments, zero exercise is always admissible. This feature is important for both the economic interpretation and the profitability arguments used later.

\paragraph{Operational constraints.}
The framework also accommodates optional operational constraints. A refraction period of length $L \in \mathbb{N}_0$ requires the holder to wait $L$ exercise dates after any strictly positive exercise:
\[
    q_i > 0 \implies q_{i+1} = \cdots = q_{i+L} = 0,
\]
with the obvious truncation near maturity. Likewise, ramping constraints of the form
\[
    |q_{i+1} - q_i| \le R
\]
can be incorporated by augmenting the state. The numerical experiments in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} focus on the baseline case with zero refraction lag and no ramping constraints, but the notation below is chosen so that these features can be activated without changing the basic formulation.

\paragraph{Payoff with convex exercise costs.}
Let $K$ denote the contractual strike and let $\gamma_c \ge 1$ be the cost exponent. Exercising $q_i$ units at time $t_i$ yields
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:payoff}
    \Pi_i(q_i) = q_i (S_{t_i} - K)^+ - c q_i^{\gamma_c},
\end{equation}
where $c \ge 0$ is the cost coefficient. The term $c q_i^{\gamma_c}$ may be interpreted as an imbalance charge, liquidity penalty, or reduced-form operational disutility. When $\gamma_c = 1$, the cost is linear and the immediate payoff is affine in $q_i$. When $\gamma_c > 1$, the cost is convex and interior optimal exercises can arise.

The use of the positive part $(S_{t_i} - K)^+$ makes the contract a \emph{call-like} swing option: exercising is purely optional and the holder never pays more than the cost component $c q_i^{\gamma_c}$. This distinguishes the present formulation from take-or-pay contracts in which the per-unit payoff is $S_{t_i} - K$ (which may be negative) and minimum-volume obligations effectively enforce exercise. Our optional-exercise assumption is natural for the profitability gate introduced in Section~\ref{sec:profitability_gate} and is standard in the financial options literature~\cite{jaillet2004valuation,carmona2008swing}.

\paragraph{Admissible controls and contract value.}
Let $\mathcal{A}_i(h_i)$ denote the set of feasible exercises at time $t_i$ given the current history $h_i$, including the per-period cap, remaining cumulative volume, and any active operational constraints. An admissible policy is a sequence of $\mathcal{F}_{t_i}$-measurable decisions $(q_i)_{i=0}^{n-1}$ with $q_i \in \mathcal{A}_i(h_i)$ almost surely. The time-zero value of the swing contract is then
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:value0}
    V_0 = \sup_{(q_i) \in \mathcal{Q}}
    \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}
    \biggl[
        \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} e^{-r t_i} \Pi_i(q_i)
        \biggr],
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Q}$ is the set of admissible policies and $r$ is the discount rate used under the chosen pricing measure. Since $t_i = i \Delta t$, it is convenient to write
\[
    \delta = e^{-r \Delta t},
    \qquad
    e^{-r t_i} = \delta^i.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:value0} is the finite-horizon stochastic control problem solved in the remainder of the paper.

\section{Classical Methods and Motivation}
\label{sec:classical_methods}

\chg{para reframed: the grid recursion is now implemented as the DP reference pricer}%
\hlblock{Before turning to reinforcement learning, it is useful to recall the classical dynamic-programming viewpoint for swing-option valuation under HHK dynamics. On the two-factor HHK testbed this recursion is not merely a conceptual foil: we implement it in full as a high-accuracy reference pricer (Section~\ref{sec:dp_reference}), whose near-exact values are used throughout Section~\ref{sec:experiments} to certify the distance of every simulation-based method from the optimum. The discussion here fixes notation and explains why the same construction degrades as model or contract complexity grows, which motivates the simulation-based approach adopted later.}

\subsection{Grid-Based Dynamic Programming}

Under the HHK model with contract bookkeeping variables, a Markov state can be written as
\[
    \begin{aligned}
        Z_i = \bigl( & X_{t_i},\,Y_{t_i},  \\
                     & Q_i,\,\rho_i\bigr),
    \end{aligned}
\]
where $\rho_i$ denotes any additional operational counter, for example the remaining refraction lag. A grid-based method approximates the continuous state space by structured grids
\[
    \begin{aligned}
        X_{t_i} & \in \{x_1,\dots,x_{N_X}\},       \\
        Y_{t_i} & \in \{y_1,\dots,y_{N_Y}\},       \\
        Q_i     & \in \{q^{(0)},\dots,q^{(N_Q)}\},
    \end{aligned}
\]
and, if required, a finite grid for the refraction state.

If the action set is also discretised, the grid Bellman recursion takes the form
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:grid_bellman}
    V_i(z) = \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}_i(z)}
    \left[
        \Pi_i(a)
        + \delta \sum_{z'} P_i(z,z';a) V_{i+1}(z')
        \right],
\end{equation}
with terminal condition $V_n \equiv 0$. Here $P_i(z,z';a)$ denotes the one-step transition probability between neighbouring grid states, including both the latent-factor dynamics and the contract bookkeeping update. The diffusion factor $X$ admits an exact Gaussian transition, while the spike factor $Y$ leads to a Poisson mixture that must be approximated numerically~\cite{Hambly2009}.

\subsection{Why Continuous-Control RL is Attractive}

\chg{block rewritten: DP is feasible/best on the testbed; limits reframed as scaling}%
\hlblock{In the baseline two-factor HHK setting none of the classical difficulties is prohibitive---our reference implementation of Section~\ref{sec:dp_reference} treats the jump mixture by quadrature and the continuous convex-cost control by a one-dimensional inner maximisation, and it is in fact the fastest and most accurate method in the study. The structural limitations appear when the setting grows beyond the testbed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exponential state scaling.} The grid cost is multiplicative in the resolution of every state variable, $O(n \cdot n_X n_Y n_Q)$ per quadrature node here. Each additional market factor (a stochastic seasonal level, a second spike regime, a forward-curve factor) or operational counter (refraction cooldown, ramping memory) multiplies the node count; simulation-based methods scale in the number of sampled paths instead.
    \item \textbf{Transition-law dependence.} The recursion consumes one-step transition densities in quadrature-friendly form. The HHK kernel is available analytically, but recalibrated, regime-switching, or empirically fitted dynamics generally offer only a simulator; a method that learns from sampled transitions applies unchanged.
    \item \textbf{Action--state coupling.} Continuous actions and convex costs are handled exactly by the inner solve in two factors, but the inner maximisation must be repeated at every node of the (growing) state grid, so the action treatment compounds the dimensionality cost rather than adding to it.
\end{itemize}
These considerations motivate a simulation-based method that works with continuous actions and sampled trajectories rather than transition densities on a grid---not as a replacement for the dynamic program where the latter is feasible, but as the route that survives model and contract growth. The reinforcement-learning formulation developed next is designed for exactly this setting, and the grid method it must be measured against is developed in Section~\ref{sec:dp_reference}.}

\section{Reinforcement Learning Formulation}
\label{sec:rl_formulation}

We now cast~\eqref{eq:value0} as a finite-horizon Markov decision process. The objective remains the discounted contract value under $\mathbb{Q}$; reinforcement learning enters only as a numerical method for approximating the optimal control.

\subsection{Markov Decision Process}
\label{sec:mdp}

At exercise time $t_i$, the underlying control problem is Markov in the HHK market factors and contract bookkeeping variables. The actor and critic do not use a minimal sufficient state directly; instead, they receive the following nine-dimensional engineered feature vector:
\[
    \begin{aligned}
        s_i = \bigl( & S_{t_i} - K,
        Q_i / Q_{\max},
        (Q_{\max} - Q_i) / Q_{\max}, \\
                     & (T - t_i)/T,
        i/n,
        S_{t_i},
        X_{t_i},
        Y_{t_i},
        \rho_i\bigr).
    \end{aligned}
\]
The display above lists the raw economic quantities; Table~\ref{tab:state_components} details the meaning and preprocessing applied to each component before network ingestion (e.g., log-moneyness for the spot and a robust-normalisation layer for the latent factors). The representation is intentionally non-minimal: the paired inventory features $Q_i/Q_{\max}$ and $(Q_{\max}-Q_i)/Q_{\max}$, as well as the paired time features $(T-t_i)/T$ and $i/n$, encode the same information up to affine transforms. Consistent with this, the time-to-maturity component is implemented as the affine surrogate $(n-i)/(n-1)$ (the fraction of decision dates remaining, counting the current one), as recorded in Table~\ref{tab:state_components}. We retain both pairs as an engineering choice because they stabilised optimisation in our experiments. This follows common DRL practice, where the economic state may be Markov in a minimal representation while the network input is allowed to be a richer feature map tailored to function approximation \cite{kolm2019dynamic,pickard2023review,cao2022gamma}. A systematic minimal-state ablation is left for future work.

We use ``model-agnostic'' throughout in a specific sense: AC-sample requires only the ability to \emph{simulate} the spot dynamics and never the transition density, so the same algorithm applies to any energy price model one can sample. It is not model-free in the broader sense: the state above includes the simulated HHK latent factors $X_{t_i}$ and $Y_{t_i}$, and the profitability gate uses the known contract payoff and cost structure. Applying the method to market data would therefore require either filtering the latent factors or a state representation built from observable quantities; we treat this as part of the controlled numerical testbed rather than a claim about plug-and-play deployment.

The actor outputs a normalised exercise
\[
    \tilde{q}_i \in [0,1],
\]
which is mapped to the physical exercise quantity by
\[
    q_i = q_{\max} \tilde{q}_i.
\]
The environment then clips this proposal to the remaining capacity:
\[
    q_i \leftarrow \min\{q_i, Q_{\max} - Q_i\}.
\]
The next state is obtained by advancing the HHK simulator over one exercise interval and updating the contract bookkeeping variables,
\[
    \begin{aligned}
        \bigl(X_{t_{i+1}},Y_{t_{i+1}}\bigr)
                & = \operatorname{SimHHK}\bigl(X_{t_i},Y_{t_i};\Delta t\bigr), \\
        Q_{i+1} & = Q_i + q_i.
    \end{aligned}
\]
We write $\operatorname{SimHHK}$ for the one-step simulation routine used on the exercise grid; it draws from the exact conditional distributions of $X$ and $Y$ under $\mathbb{Q}$. The presentation below does not rely on a particular discretisation scheme, only on the ability to simulate the model consistently under the pricing measure.

\paragraph{Variance reduction in path generation.}
The implementation uses scrambled Sobol quasi-Monte Carlo draws for the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck increments of $X$, improving the uniformity of path coverage relative to plain pseudo-random sampling. All reported valuation estimates are then computed on a large common test sample of $65{,}536$ paths so that Monte Carlo noise remains small relative to the RL--LSM differences reported below.

The replay buffer stores the pre-discounted one-step reward
\[
    \tilde R_i(q_i) = \delta^i \Pi_i(q_i),
    \qquad
    \delta = e^{-r\Delta t},
\]
so that the RL objective is
\[
    J(\pi) =
    \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}
    \biggl[
        \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \tilde R_i(q_i)
        \biggr]
    =
    \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}
    \biggl[
        \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \delta^i \Pi_i(q_i)
        \biggr].
\]
This is exactly the swing-option value~\eqref{eq:value0} for the policy class represented by the actor. Using pre-discounted rewards lets the RL recursion operate with $\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}} = 1$ without changing the financial objective.

\subsection{Enforcement of Swing Constraints}

The contract constraints enter the RL formulation through the environment and the action post-processing.

\paragraph{Per-period cap and cumulative cap.}
The actor proposes a normalised action in $[0,1]$, which corresponds to a physical exercise in $[0,q_{\max}]$. The environment then enforces the cumulative cap by clipping to the remaining capacity $Q_{\max} - Q_i$.

\paragraph{Refraction constraints.}
The state variable $\rho_i$, taking values in $[0,1]$, records the normalised number of steps elapsed since the last exercise and acts as a soft proximity signal for refraction-style constraints. If a mandatory refraction period is active, the environment forces $q_i = 0$ while a cooldown counter is positive. Then $\rho_i$ gives the policy a continuous summary of how recently the last exercise occurred. In the reported experiments no refraction is imposed, so $\rho_i$ carries elapsed-since-exercise information only. Retaining it in the state nevertheless makes the extension to richer contracts explicit.

\paragraph{Profitability masking.}
For the contract class studied numerically, zero exercise is always admissible and only an upper cumulative volume cap is imposed. Consequently, any action with strictly negative immediate payoff is weakly dominated by zero exercise: it lowers the current cash flow and consumes flexibility that could be used later. In this setting the profitability gate introduced in Section~\ref{sec:profitability_gate} is therefore lossless rather than merely heuristic. Operationally this profitability projection is applied at the environment level and shared by every method: after feasibility clipping, the environment and evaluator map any exercise with non-positive net payoff to zero for both the actor--critic policy and the LSM-D baseline, so the comparison in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} treats the two methods symmetrically. We do not claim the same argument without modification for contracts with mandatory take obligations, active ramping constraints, or hard refraction limits. Under such path-dependent restrictions, it may be optimal to accept a small immediate loss in order to preserve feasibility for a more valuable future exercise, so the hard gate would need to be relaxed.

\subsection{Actor--Critic Algorithm}
\label{sec:actor_critic}

We use a deterministic actor--critic architecture~\cite{silver2014dpg} with target networks and uniform replay. The actor $\pi_\theta : \mathcal{S} \to [0,1]$ maps states to normalised exercise quantities, while the critic $Q_\phi : \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \to \mathbb{R}$ estimates pre-discounted continuation values. The implementation supports two critic targets. AC-sample uses the standard single-sample temporal-difference bootstrap and requires only simulated state transitions. AC-kernel replaces that bootstrap with a conditional expectation over the HHK transition kernel, using the model-specific structure to reduce target variance.

\subsubsection{Deterministic Policy Gradient}

The actor is trained by ascending the critic value~\cite{silver2014dpg},
\[
    \nabla_\theta J(\theta)
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \mathcal{D}}
    \left[
        \nabla_a Q_\phi(s,a)\big|_{a=\pi_\theta(s)}
        \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(s)
        \right],
\]
where $\mathcal{D}$ denotes the replay buffer. Target networks $(\pi_{\theta'},Q_{\phi'})$ are updated by Polyak averaging,
\[
    \theta' \leftarrow (1-\tau)\theta' + \tau \theta,
    \qquad
    \phi' \leftarrow (1-\tau)\phi' + \tau \phi,
\]
with $\tau = 0.0032$.

\subsubsection{Critic Loss}

The critic is scalar-valued. In AC-sample it is trained with the one-step temporal-difference target
\[
    y_{\mathrm{sample}} = \tilde R + (1-d) Q_{\phi'}\bigl(s',\pi_{\theta'}(s')\bigr),
\]
where $d$ is the terminal indicator and $\tilde R$ is the pre-discounted one-step reward stored in the replay buffer. In AC-kernel the sampled continuation value is replaced by
\[
    y_{\mathrm{kernel}} =
    \tilde R
    + (1-d)
      \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{HHK}}}
      \!\left[
          Q_{\phi'}\bigl(s^+,\pi_{\theta'}(s^+)\bigr)
          \,\middle|\, s,a
      \right],
\]
where $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{HHK}}$ is the exact one-step HHK transition law on the exercise grid. The expectation is evaluated with shared Gauss--Hermite nodes for the Gaussian $X$ transition and a fast quadrature mesh for the rapidly decaying spike factor $Y$. The loss for either target is the unweighted mean squared TD error
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{critic}}
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{(s,a,\tilde R,s',d) \sim \mathcal{D}}
    \Bigl[
        \bigl(y - Q_\phi(s,a)\bigr)^2
        \Bigr],
\end{equation*}
with $y=y_{\mathrm{sample}}$ for AC-sample and $y=y_{\mathrm{kernel}}$ for AC-kernel.

\subsubsection{Uniform Replay}

Transitions are stored in a circular replay buffer and sampled uniformly; prioritized replay is not used in either reported recipe. The buffer capacity and the minimum fill before updates begin are variant-specific: AC-kernel uses a capacity of $10^5$ transitions and starts updates after $1{,}000$ stored transitions, while AC-sample uses a larger capacity of $2\times10^5$ and starts after $18{,}000$. The two variants differ in target-policy smoothing: AC-kernel uses none, whereas AC-sample injects a small Gaussian noise into the target action (standard deviation $0.15$, decaying to $0.04$ over training) before evaluating the bootstrap target, following the original single-sample recipe.

\subsection{Profitability-Gated Actor}
\label{sec:profitability_gate}

Convex exercise costs imply that large exercises can be locally or globally unattractive even when the option is in the money. The actor therefore includes a hard gate that clips proposed exercises to quantities with non-negative immediate payoff.

\subsubsection{Profitability Region}

Fix a time $t_i$ and write
\[
    h_i = (S_{t_i} - K)^+.
\]
Then the immediate payoff is
\[
    \Pi_i(q) = h_i q - c q^{\gamma_c}.
\]
When $c > 0$ and $\gamma_c > 1$, the largest exercise that still yields non-negative immediate payoff is obtained from
\[
    h_i q = c q^{\gamma_c},
\]
namely
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:qbar}
    \bar{q}_i =
    \left(
    \frac{h_i}{c}
    \right)^{1/(\gamma_c - 1)}.
\end{equation}
Therefore $\Pi_i(q) \ge 0$ for every $q \in [0,\bar{q}_i]$ and $\Pi_i(q) < 0$ for every $q > \bar{q}_i$. The stationary point of the immediate payoff is
\[
    q_i^{\mathrm{stat}} =
    \left(
    \frac{h_i}{c \gamma_c}
    \right)^{1/(\gamma_c - 1)},
\]
which lies below $\bar{q}_i$ and explains why interior optima arise under convex costs. The gate uses the zero-profit threshold $\bar{q}_i$, not the stationary point.

\subsubsection{Hard Gate Mechanism}

Let $\tilde{q}_i \in [0,1]$ be the raw actor output. The profitability gate clips it to
\[
    \tilde{q}_i^{\mathrm{gate}} =
    \min\!\left(
    \tilde{q}_i,
    \frac{\bar{q}_i}{q_{\max}},
    1
    \right)
\]
when $c > 0$ and $\gamma_c > 1$. For linear costs ($\gamma_c = 1$), the immediate payoff is affine in $q$ and non-negative exercise is possible only when $h_i \ge c$; otherwise the gate sets the action to zero.

\subsubsection{Straight-Through Estimator}

The clipping operation is non-differentiable, so we use a straight-through estimator:
\[
    \tilde{q}_i^{\mathrm{out}}
    =
    \tilde{q}_i + \sg\!\left(\tilde{q}_i^{\mathrm{gate}} - \tilde{q}_i\right).
\]
In the forward pass, $\tilde{q}_i^{\mathrm{out}} = \tilde{q}_i^{\mathrm{gate}}$; in the backward pass, gradients flow through the unclipped proposal $\tilde{q}_i$~\cite{bengio2013ste}. This retains a simple differentiable training signal while ensuring that the executed action satisfies the profitability bound. The STE introduces a biased gradient estimator; empirically, the resulting policies remain stable across the reported experiments and seeds.

\subsubsection{Implementation}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:profitability_gate} summarises the gated actor forward pass.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
    \caption{Profitability-gated actor forward pass}
    \label{alg:profitability_gate}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require State $s_i$ with $(S_{t_i} - K)$ at index $0$
        \Require Cost parameters $c$, $\gamma_c$, $q_{\max}$
        \Ensure Feasible normalised action $\tilde{q}_i^{\mathrm{out}} \in [0,1]$
        \State $u \gets \operatorname{MLP}(s_i)$
        \State $\tilde{q}_i \gets \operatorname{sig}(\beta_{\mathrm{act}} u)$ \Comment{$\beta_{\mathrm{act}}$ is variant-specific}
        \If{$c > 0$ and $\gamma_c > 1$}
        \State $\bar{q}_i \gets \bigl((S_{t_i}-K)^+/c\bigr)^{1/(\gamma_c-1)}$
        \State $\tilde{q}_i^{\max} \gets \min\{1,\bar{q}_i/q_{\max}\}$
        \State $\tilde{q}_i^{\mathrm{gate}} \gets \min\{\tilde{q}_i,\tilde{q}_i^{\max}\}$
        \ElsIf{$c > 0$ and $\gamma_c = 1$ and $(S_{t_i}-K)^+ < c$}
        \State $\tilde{q}_i^{\mathrm{gate}} \gets 0$
        \Else
        \State $\tilde{q}_i^{\mathrm{gate}} \gets \tilde{q}_i$
        \EndIf
        \State \Return $\tilde{q}_i + \sg(\tilde{q}_i^{\mathrm{gate}} - \tilde{q}_i)$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Exploration Strategy}
\label{sec:exploration}

Exploration is challenging in continuous action spaces because the actor output is squashed to $[0,1]$. We therefore inject Gaussian noise before the output nonlinearity:
\[
    u_i' = u_i + \sigma_{\mathrm{expl}} \epsilon_i,
    \qquad
    \epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1).
\]
Pre-squash noise remains effective even when the policy is close to the action bounds. The two variants use frozen exploration recipes. AC-kernel uses a linear decay from $\sigma_0=1.30$ to $\sigma_{\mathrm{floor}}=0.26$ over the training horizon. AC-sample uses the sample-target recipe with an initial plateau followed by hyperbolic decay,
\[
    \sigma_{\mathrm{expl}}(e) =
    \begin{cases}
        \sigma_0, & e < e_{\mathrm{plateau}},   \\[0.3em]
        \sigma_{\mathrm{floor}} +
        \dfrac{\sigma_0 - \sigma_{\mathrm{floor}}}
        {1 + \dfrac{e-e_{\mathrm{plateau}}}{e_{\mathrm{plateau}}}},
                  & e \ge e_{\mathrm{plateau}},
    \end{cases}
\]
with $e_{\mathrm{plateau}}$ set to $10\%$ of the episode budget.
We additionally scale the pre-squash noise standard deviation adaptively as
\[
    \begin{aligned}
        \tilde\sigma_i
         & = \sigma_{\mathrm{expl}}(e)             \\
         & \quad \cdot \bigl(1 + 0.6\,|u_i|\bigr),
    \end{aligned}
\]
where $u_i$ is the pre-squash logit output by the actor before the $\beta$-sigmoid nonlinearity.
This increases the effective noise magnitude when the logit is large (i.e.\ the policy is saturated near the action bounds), where the sigmoid gradient is small and unscaled noise would have little effect on the squashed output.

During the critic warmup, actor updates are frozen and only the critic is trained. The warmup length is variant-specific: $512$ episodes for AC-kernel and $1{,}024$ episodes for AC-sample. AC-kernel uses no target-policy smoothing; AC-sample adds the small decaying target-policy noise described in Section~\ref{sec:actor_critic}. Target-action clipping is unused in both variants.

\paragraph{Evaluation weight averaging.}
Both variants maintain a shadow copy of the actor weights as an exponential moving average (EMA) over training with decay $0.999$: after each actor update, the EMA copy is updated by $\bar\theta \leftarrow 0.999\,\bar\theta + 0.001\,\theta$. All held-out evaluations use the EMA actor $\pi_{\bar\theta}$ rather than the live actor $\pi_\theta$. This averaging has no effect on gradient computation and does not alter the training dynamics; it acts as a lightweight Polyak average of the actor trajectory and reduces sensitivity to late-training noise in the evaluation signal.

\subsection{Neural Network Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

Both actor and critic are lightweight multilayer perceptrons with LayerNorm on every hidden layer. The actor takes the $9$-dimensional state of Table~\ref{tab:state_components} and the critic appends the scalar action ($10$ inputs); both emit a scalar output (exercise quantity and continuation value, respectively). The two variants differ slightly in capacity: AC-sample uses two width-$64$ hidden layers for both networks, while AC-kernel uses two width-$32$ layers for the actor and four width-$48$ layers for the deeper critic.

The actor output uses $\operatorname{sig}(\beta_{\mathrm{act}} u)$, where $\operatorname{sig}$ denotes the logistic sigmoid. The two variants use different hidden activations: AC-sample uses the SiLU gate $x\,\sigma(x)$, while AC-kernel uses a steeper swish gate $x\,\sigma(3x)$ (swish-$\beta$3). The steeper gate was retained for AC-kernel because, paired with the low-variance expected target, it improved the focal value at negligible cost; under the noisier single-sample target it gave no benefit, so AC-sample keeps the standard SiLU. State preprocessing is domain-specific and shared across variants: the spot component is mapped to log-moneyness $\log(S_{t_i}/K)$; the latent factors $X_{t_i}$ and $Y_{t_i}$ are passed through a robust-normalisation layer based on median/IQR scaling. In the reported recipes this layer uses default identity statistics (median $0$, IQR $1$) rather than per-dataset fitted values, so the latent factors enter the network essentially unscaled apart from the hidden-layer normalisation. The hidden-layer initialisation differs by variant---AC-sample uses orthogonal weights and AC-kernel uses He (scaled-Gaussian) weights---and both use a small uniform initialisation drawn from $[-0.003,0.003]$ for the output layers.

Training uses AdamW. The actor weight decay is $5 \times 10^{-5}$ in both recipes; the critic weight decay is $10^{-4}$ for AC-kernel and $1.2 \times 10^{-4}$ for AC-sample. AC-kernel uses constant learning rates $\eta_a = 3.0 \times 10^{-4}$ and $\eta_c = 5.0 \times 10^{-4}$. AC-sample uses smaller base rates $\eta_a = 1.6 \times 10^{-4}$ and $\eta_c = 9.0 \times 10^{-5}$ under a cosine decay schedule with a short linear warmup; it additionally applies a duplicated critic optimiser step per update (an effective doubling of the critic learning rate), following the original single-sample recipe.

\subsection{Training Procedure}
\label{sec:training}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:training} gives the training loop used for both actor--critic variants.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
    \caption{Actor--critic training for swing-option pricing\chg{fix: replay capacity/min-fill are variant-specific (hyperparameter table), not the AC-kernel values}}
    \label{alg:training}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require Swing contract $\mathcal{C}$, HHK parameters $\Theta_{\mathrm{HHK}}$
        \Require Training episodes $N_{\mathrm{train}}$, held-out test paths $N_{\mathrm{test}}$
        \Require Variant $v \in \{\text{AC-sample},\text{AC-kernel}\}$
        \Ensure Saved final actor checkpoint and metadata
        \State Generate training paths $(t,S,X,Y)$
        \Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent}$\gets \operatorname{SimHHK}(\Theta_{\mathrm{HHK}},N_{\mathrm{train}})$
        \State Generate a disjoint held-out evaluation set
        \Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent}$\gets \operatorname{SimHHK}(\Theta_{\mathrm{HHK}},N_{\mathrm{test}})$
        \State Initialise actor $\pi_\theta$, critic $Q_\phi$, and targets $\pi_{\theta'}$, $Q_{\phi'}$
        \State Initialise uniform replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$ \hlc{(variant-specific capacity, Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams})}
        \State Initialise actor bias by the closed-form myopic first-order condition
        \State If $v=\text{AC-kernel}$, precompute the shared HHK transition quadrature
        \Statex \textbf{// Main RL training loop}
        \For{episode $e = 1$ to $N_{\mathrm{train}}$}
        \State $s_0 \gets \operatorname{Env.Reset}(\text{path\_idx}=e)$

        \State $\sigma_{\mathrm{expl}} \gets \operatorname{NoiseSchedule}(e)$
        \State $u_i \gets \operatorname{ActorPreAct}(s_i)$

        \State $a_i \gets \operatorname{GateAndClip}(\tilde{q}_i,s_i)$
        \State $s_{i+1}, \tilde R_i, \mathrm{done} \gets \operatorname{Env.Step}(a_i)$
        \State $\mathcal{D}.\operatorname{Add}(s_i,a_i,\tilde R_i,s_{i+1},\mathrm{done})$

        \State Sample $(s,a,\tilde R,s',d)$ uniformly from $\mathcal{D}$
        \State Update $Q_\phi$ with $y_{\mathrm{sample}}$ or $y_{\mathrm{kernel}}$
        \If{critic warmup has elapsed}
        \State Update $\pi_\theta$ with the deterministic policy gradient
        \State Soft-update $\pi_{\theta'}$
        \EndIf

        \EndIf
        \EndFor
        \State Optionally record a held-out evaluation diagnostic every $1{,}024$ episodes
        \EndFor

    \end{algorithmic}

\paragraph{Closed-form actor initialisation.}
To avoid the early episodes where an uninitialised actor proposes near-zero exercises regardless of spot price, the actor output bias is set so that the initial policy approximates the myopic (single-period) unconstrained optimum. Setting $\partial\Pi_i(q)/\partial q = 0$ under convex costs $(c>0,\,\gamma_c>1)$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:myopic_stationary}
    q_i^{\mathrm{stat}} = \left(\frac{(S_{t_i}-K)^+}{c\,\gamma_c}\right)^{1/(\gamma_c-1)},
\end{equation}
and the warm-start target clips this to the admissible range and a budget-proportional ceiling:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:warmstart_target}
    q^*(S)
    =
    \operatorname{clip}\!\Bigl(
        q_i^{\mathrm{stat}},\;
        q_{\min},\;
        \min\!\bigl\{q_{\max},\,Q_{\max}/n\bigr\}
    \Bigr).
\end{equation}
This target is averaged over a set of warmup spot realisations drawn from the training paths. The resulting mean normalised target $\bar{\tilde{q}}^* \in (0,1)$ is converted to a pre-squash logit by $\bar u = \operatorname{sig}^{-1}(\bar{\tilde{q}}^*)/\beta_{\mathrm{act}}$, and the actor output bias is initialised to $\bar u$. The procedure is $O(1)$ in network parameters and adds no training episodes; it is used identically by both AC-sample and AC-kernel.

\subsection{Discretized-Action LSM Baseline (LSM-D)}
\label{sec:lsm}

For comparison we use a least-squares Monte Carlo baseline that discretizes the exercise decision into $M$ equally spaced levels on $[0,q_{\max}]$ with spacing $\Delta q = q_{\max}/(M-1)$, and approximates continuation values by Longstaff--Schwartz regression~\cite{longstaff2001valuing}. With $M = 5$ and $q_{\max} = 2.0$, the action grid is $\{0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0\}$.

To keep the comparison economically fair, the LSM-D policy conditions on the same market information as the RL agent: the continuation regression uses the HHK market state $(S_{t_i},X_{t_i},Y_{t_i})$, while inventory and cooldown are handled as discrete state slices. Because the exercise quantity is discretized, the dynamic-programming state tracks remaining cumulative capacity in multiples of $\Delta q$. The number of inventory levels is therefore $\lfloor Q_{\max}/\Delta q \rfloor + 1 = 41$ for $Q_{\max} = 20$ and $\Delta q = 0.5$. Together with the cooldown state, each time step requires $M - 1$ continuation-value regressions per inventory--cooldown pair for the non-zero actions, plus one for the hold action.

At each exercise date $t_i$, regression features are normalised to $[-1,1]$ using cross-path min--max scaling. Continuation values are regressed with a degree-$2$ total-degree Chebyshev basis on $(S_{t_i}, X_{t_i}, Y_{t_i})$ (the tensor Chebyshev basis truncated to total degree two) and no regularisation. The exercise rule selects the action $m^* \in \{0,1,\ldots,M-1\}$ that maximises the sum of the immediate net payoff and fitted continuation value:
\[
    m^* = \arg\max_{m}\; \Pi_i(q_m) + \widehat{C}_i^{(m)}(S_{t_i},X_{t_i},Y_{t_i}),
\]
where $q_0 = 0$ denotes holding and $\widehat{C}_i^{(m)}$ is the fitted continuation value after exercising quantity $q_m$, subject to the constraint that $q_m$ does not exceed the remaining capacity. At the terminal date, exercise occurs only if the net payoff $\Pi_{n-1}(q_m)$ is positive. With $M = 2$ the algorithm reduces to the classical bang-bang case; with $M = 5$ it can select from five volume levels at each step, permitting graduated exercise under convex costs.

The LSM-D basis and action grid are fixed a priori across configurations, so no validation-based model selection is used for the classical benchmark. For each parameter configuration, the regression coefficients are fit on an independent training sample of $32{,}768$ paths and the resulting policy is then evaluated without re-fitting on the same per-seed out-of-sample sets used for the RL results (each training seed $k$ is priced on its own seed-$(k+777)$ test set of $N_{\mathrm{test}} = 65{,}536$ paths).

We also tested a reduced-state variant that conditions only on the current spot price using a degree-$7$ Chebyshev basis. Across the reported configurations, the full-state regression yields on average approximately $0.4\%$ higher option values, confirming that conditioning on the latent factors modestly improves continuation-value fits; this margin is smaller than the gain from moving from $M = 2$ to $M = 5$, suggesting that action-space resolution is the more important lever for the LSM-D baseline.

\subsection{A Near-Exact Dynamic-Programming Reference}
\label{sec:dp_reference}
\chg{ENTIRE SUBSECTION 5.2 IS NEW: DP solver, verification, numerical-uncertainty bands, focal head-to-head (Table 3) and convergence/Pareto figure (Figure 1)}

\hlblock{Because the HHK model is two-factor with an exactly Gaussian OU transition and a compound-Poisson spike, the grid Bellman recursion~\eqref{eq:grid_bellman} of Section~\ref{sec:classical_methods} is directly implementable on this testbed. We do so and use the resulting values $V^{\mathrm{DP}}$ as a near-exact reference against which all three simulation-based methods are measured. The solver performs backward induction on a tensor-product grid over the market factors $(X,Y)$ and the cumulative volume $Q$: the one-step conditional expectation is computed from the analytical HHK transition kernel by Gauss--Hermite quadrature in the OU factor and a Poisson-mixture quadrature in the spike factor, values are transferred between grid levels by not-a-knot cubic splines, and the continuous convex-cost control is resolved at every node by a bracketed one-dimensional maximisation. For payoffs linear in the exercise quantity ($\gamma_c=1$ or $c=0$) the optimal control is bang-bang and the value function is piecewise linear in $Q$ with kinks only on the lattice $\{k\,q_{\max}\}$~\cite{bardou2010bangbang}; since $Q_{\max}/q_{\max}$ is integral here, these cells are solved on the exact eleven-node volume lattice, which removes the $Q$-discretisation error identically and leaves only spatial and quadrature error.}

\hlpar{Verification.}{The implementation is verified at three levels. First, the quadrature kernel reproduces the closed-form one-step moments of the HHK transition and an independent reference implementation of the analytical kernel to below $10^{-9}$. Second, limiting cases are recovered: the zero-volatility contract matches the exact Lagrangian budget allocation, the price is monotone in $c$, $q_{\max}$, $Q_{\max}$, and $\sigma$, and the linear-cost corner is bang-bang. (The price is \emph{not} globally monotone in $\gamma_c$: at high cost the optimum moves interior with $q<1$, where $c\,q^{\gamma_c}$ decreases in $\gamma_c$, so $V^{\mathrm{DP}}$ at $(c,\gamma_c)=(0.15,3)$ exceeds its $\gamma_c=2$ neighbour---an economic effect, not a solver artifact.) Third, and decisively, rolling the greedy DP policy forward on independent out-of-sample HHK paths gives an unbiased value estimate of an implementable policy, hence a true lower bound on the optimum: at the focal cell a $10^8$-path forward evaluation yields $1.99018$ (standard error $2.2\times10^{-4}$), i.e.\ $1.5\times10^{-4}$ below the backward value of the grid that produced the policy and within $0.7$ Monte~Carlo standard errors; spot checks at four further cells (both lattice and ladder regimes, $4\times10^6$ paths each) show the same pattern, the forward value approaching the backward reference from below within $1.3$--$1.7$ standard errors.}

\hlpar{Numerical uncertainty.}{The DP value carries no statistical uncertainty; its error is deterministic discretisation error, which we quantify per cell in the standard verification-and-validation manner~\cite{roache1994perspective,asme2009vv20,eca2014procedure}. For each convex-cost cell, a geometric three-grid refinement of the controlling volume axis $n_Q$ (at spatial and quadrature resolutions whose own residuals are $\le 3\times10^{-5}$) yields an observed convergence order (between $1.3$ and $3.0$ across cells; the corner cell $(0.15,3)$ is already converged below the ladder resolution and is banded by its last-rung spread) and a Richardson-extrapolated limit, and the reported band is the Grid Convergence Index $U_{\mathrm{num}} = 1.25\,(|V_{\mathrm{ext}}-V^{\mathrm{DP}}| + U_{\mathrm{spatial}})$. For the exact-lattice cells the band is $1.25\times$ the oscillation envelope of the four finest members of a six-grid spatial ladder (the bang-bang exercise boundary is a kink in the value surface, so spline convergence is oscillatory there). Across all $29$ cells the resulting $U_{\mathrm{num}}$ ranges from $3.8\times10^{-5}$ to $1.9\times10^{-4}$ in absolute value terms ($4.5\times10^{-5}$ at the focal cell)---one to two orders of magnitude below the $95\%$ BCa half-widths of the Monte~Carlo methods in Table~\ref{tab:results} ($\approx 6\times10^{-3}$). \emph{The DP reference can therefore be read as exact relative to every statistical comparison in this paper: the distance of a method from $V^{\mathrm{DP}}$ inherits that method's own confidence interval unchanged.} Table~\ref{tab:dp_headtohead} summarises the focal-cell head-to-head; Figure~\ref{fig:dp_convergence} shows the convergence study and the accuracy--time frontier.}

\subsection{Analysis of Exercise Behavior}
\label{sec:exercise_behavior}

To quantify the degree to which a policy adheres to the all-or-nothing exercise decisions typical of linear cost structures, we introduce the \emph{Bang-Bangness} metric $B$. We define $B$ as the proportion of feasible full-capacity exercises that actually hit the maximum allowable quantity. Specifically, over the subset of steps where the remaining capacity is at least the maximum single-step limit ($Q_{\max} - Q_i \ge q_{\max}$) and the agent chooses to exercise a strictly positive amount ($q_t > 0$), the metric is
\begin{equation}
    B = \mathbb{P}\left( \frac{q_t}{q_{\max}} \ge 0.95 \;\middle|\; Q_{\max} - Q_i \ge q_{\max},\, q_t > 0 \right).
\end{equation}
A bang-bang policy yields $B=100\%$, while a completely uniform random exercise decision over $(0,q_{\max}]$ yields an expected baseline of $B \approx 5\%$. The threshold $0.95$ is heuristic rather than structural; we verified that the qualitative monotone decline in $B$ as convexity increases is robust to the threshold choice across $k \in [0,1]$.

Figure~\ref{fig:spot_income_pv_hist} provides a microscopic view of the exercise behavior under both methods. The left column scatters the non-discounted one-step net exercise profit against the underlying spot price. When $c>0$ and costs are linear, both the actor--critic policy and the discretized-action LSM-D baseline remain close to the same bang-bang frontier, confirming the expected all-or-nothing structure.

Under convex costs ($\gamma_c \in \{1.5,2.0\}$), a wide region of profitable partial exercise opens beneath the strict bang-bang boundary. Both methods exploit this region: LSM-D does so through five discrete volume levels, while the actor--critic policy continuously modulates its exercise quantity and therefore produces a denser interior cloud of partial exercises.

The right column of Figure~\ref{fig:spot_income_pv_hist} shows the corresponding distribution of the path-level present value of exercise costs. The Bang-Bangness values reported in the panel titles fall sharply as convexity increases, and they are typically slightly lower for the continuous RL policy than for LSM-D, reflecting the smoother interior exercise made possible by continuous control.

Figure~\ref{fig:bang_bangness_trend} traces this trend across the cost grid. For every cost coefficient, $B$ declines monotonically as the convexity exponent $\gamma_c$ rises, and it falls faster at larger $c$: at $\gamma_c=1$ the continuous AC-kernel policy is already moderately graduated ($B\approx0.66$--$0.74$) against a near-perfectly bang-bang LSM-D ($B\approx1$), and by $\gamma_c=2$ both methods exercise well inside full capacity, with the high-cost regimes ($c=0.15$) dropping below $10\%$. The AC-kernel curve sits at or below LSM-D throughout, confirming that continuous control fills the interior more finely than a five-level grid; the gap is descriptive of exercise style and does not by itself drive the small linear-cost valuation difference.

\subsection{Discussion}

Three patterns stand out in Table~\ref{tab:results} and Figure~\ref{fig:spot_income_pv_hist}.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The model-agnostic AC-sample is competitive but trails LSM-D.} Across the grid its mean $\Delta\%$ is $-0.5\%$ (median $-0.9\%$): it sits modestly below LSM-D in most cells and exceeds it in $6$ of $28$, all concentrated in the high-cost, high-convexity corner where interior exercise dominates and the coarse $M=5$ grid under-resolves. The largest deficit is $-2.3\%$ at $c=0.15$, $\gamma_c=1$, where exercise quality matters most and a single-sample critic target is noisiest; the widest confidence intervals likewise occur at high cost (e.g.\ $[-4.85,-1.32]$ at $c=0.15$, $\gamma_c=1$). AC-sample is therefore a strong, near-LSM comparator that relies on nothing beyond simulated transitions.
    \item \textbf{AC-kernel matches LSM-D under low convexity and exceeds the coarse $M=5$ grid under high convexity.} Under linear costs ($\gamma_c=1$) AC-kernel reproduces LSM-D to within $-0.19\%$ to $-0.14\%$, as expected where the optimum is bang-bang and LSM-D is near-exact \chg{DP-certification recast of this bullet}\hlt{(its certified gap to \mbox{$V^{\mathrm{DP}}$} averages just \mbox{$-0.05\%$} there)}. As convexity increases, AC-kernel rises above the reported $M=5$ benchmark: the mean $\Delta\%$ reaches $+0.24\%$ ($\gamma_c=1.5$), $+0.79\%$ ($\gamma_c=2$) and $+2.03\%$ ($\gamma_c=3$), exceeding the $M=5$ grid in $15$ of $28$ cells and by up to $+5.14\%$ ($c=0.15$, $\gamma_c=3$).
    \hlblock{The DP reference disambiguates what this gap means: the $M=5$ baseline is certifiably below the optimum under convexity (mean gaps $-0.48\%$ to $-2.14\%$ as $\gamma_c$ rises, up to $-5.1\%$), while AC-kernel stays within $0.13$--$0.30\%$ of $V^{\mathrm{DP}}$ in every cell---so under convex costs the learned policy genuinely is closer to the true optimum than the reported baseline. At the same time, the action-grid sensitivity in Table~\ref{tab:lsm_grid} shows a finer LSM-D grid ($M=9,17$) recovers most of that distance, so the advantage is one of \emph{grid-free attainment} of the continuous-exercise value rather than of the policy class per se: AC-kernel reaches within $0.3\%$ of the optimum without choosing an action grid, which the coarse fixed grid demonstrably under-resolves.}
    \item \textbf{The AC-kernel recipe improves both accuracy and stability.} Relative to AC-sample, it raises the value by $0.6$--$2.9\%$ of the LSM-D price, and its relative performance is markedly more reproducible across seeds: the paired-$\Delta\%$ BCa intervals are roughly an order of magnitude narrower than AC-sample's (half-widths $\approx 0.02$--$0.12$ versus $\approx 0.1$--$1.8$ percentage points; Table~\ref{tab:results}), so the expected target removes most of the seed-to-seed dependence in the benchmark gap. The AC-kernel recipe differs from AC-sample in more than the critic target---network depth, critic learning rate, gradient steps per interaction, warm-up length, exploration schedule, actor temperature, and weight averaging (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams})---so the headline grid alone does not isolate the target's contribution. A matched focal ablation that flips only the expected-target flag while holding the recipe fixed ($c=0.04$, $\gamma_c=2$; $4{,}096$ episodes, $12$ seeds) indicates that the expected target is the primary driver, with a focal gain of about $+2.5$ percentage points; we report this as focal rather than grid-wide evidence (Appendix~\ref{appendix:reproducibility}). We therefore read the model-agnostic deficit as largely a critic-variance and exercise-quality effect rather than a limitation of the continuous-control policy class.
\end{enumerate}

Taken together, the three-way evidence sharpens the earlier ``competitive, not dominant'' reading. The bang-bangness diagnostic is consistent with this reading: under linear costs LSM-D is fully bang-bang ($B\approx 1$) while the continuous actor--critic spreads exercise somewhat ($B\approx 0.66$--$0.74$ across the linear-cost grid, eight-seed mean); under convexity all methods move to graduated exercise (Figure~\ref{fig:spot_income_pv_hist}) and the continuous policies fill the interior more finely. We treat $B$ as a descriptive measure of exercise style rather than as the cause of the small linear-cost gap. The practical reading is that AC-sample offers a simulator-only continuous-control alternative at a small valuation cost, while AC-kernel converts a known transition law into a low-variance, seed-stable critic target that recovers the value AC-sample leaves on the table and matches a well-resolved LSM-D benchmark.

\hlblock{Several caveats apply. \subsection{Seed Robustness}
\label{sec:seed_robustness}

The paired-$\Delta\%$ confidence intervals in Table~\ref{tab:results} double as a grid-wide robustness summary: because each of the eight seeds $\{11,\dots,18\}$ is priced against LSM-D on its own \emph{common} per-seed test set, the BCa bootstrap of $\Delta\%$ absorbs both initialisation and Monte~Carlo variation and measures how reproducibly each variant reaches its benchmark gap. The expected target removes almost all dependence on the random seed: AC-kernel's intervals are tight (half-widths $\approx 0.02$--$0.12$ percentage points across the grid), whereas AC-sample's are an order of magnitude wider (up to $\approx 1.8$ pp at high cost). The tight AC-kernel intervals confirm that its near-LSM valuation is reproducible to well within the test-set uncertainty and not an artefact of a favourable seed draw, while AC-sample relies more heavily on the seed averaging used throughout.

\subsection{Computational Efficiency}

The computational burden is shifted from repeated backward induction to one-off policy training, and that training is itself inexpensive. The reported prices are produced by a hand-optimised float32 C++ reimplementation of the actor--critic pricer (Accelerate BLAS for every layer GEMM in both passes, vectorised fast-exponential activations, and a hand-derived analytic backward), which is roughly $8.8\times$ faster end-to-end than the equivalent PyTorch-eager baseline: on a single Apple~M1 core a complete $4{,}096$-episode train-and-price plus a $65{,}536$-path out-of-sample valuation takes about $43$ seconds, versus about $381$ seconds for the Python reference. The float32 price is statistically indistinguishable from the double-precision Python implementation (Welch two-sample test, $p=0.37$), and out-of-sample valuation of the $65{,}536$-path test set is sub-second. For context, the reference Python implementation trains the full $32{,}768$-episode budget in roughly $2$--$3$ hours on a $4$-core CPU.
\chg{sentence added: DP also dominates on wall-clock on this testbed}%
\hlblock{It bears repeating that on this two-factor testbed the grid-DP reference of Section~\ref{sec:dp_reference} dominates every learning-based method on speed as well as accuracy---it prices a cell to $10^{-3}$ accuracy in $\approx 0.3$ s on the same machine (Table~\ref{tab:dp_headtohead}); the case for the simulation-based methods is scalability and model-agnosticism, not testbed wall-clock.}

The expected target also buys \emph{sample} efficiency, not just wall-clock. Figure~\ref{fig:sample_efficiency} prices both actor--critic variants and the LSM-D benchmark at the focal cell as a function of training budget---episodes for the actor--critic methods, fit paths for LSM-D---over the eight seeds with bootstrap bands. AC-kernel reaches the LSM-D benchmark within about $2{,}000$ episodes and then tracks the classical dynamic program's own fit-path efficiency curve, whereas the model-agnostic AC-sample is still about $1.2\%$ below the benchmark at the full $32{,}768$-episode budget. The kernel therefore attains the benchmark value at roughly an order of magnitude fewer interactions than the model-agnostic variant.

A usable pricer must also deliver risk sensitivities and support hedging. We therefore complement the valuation study with a risk analysis at the focal regime ($c=0.04$, $\gamma_c=2$), comparing three pricers throughout: the LSM-D benchmark, the model-agnostic actor--critic (\emph{RL}, the AC-sample policy), and a kernel-augmented actor--critic (\emph{RL-kernel}, the AC-kernel policy), the model-specific extension that replaces the single-sample critic target with a variance-reduced expectation computed analytically over the HHK transition kernel. The two learned policies are the seed-$11$ focal checkpoints, evaluated frozen and without retraining for the hedging task. This is a focal risk study with its own evaluation protocol: sensitivities and hedges are computed out-of-sample on dedicated path bundles ($8{,}192$ valuation paths and $4{,}096$ hedging paths), with the two learned policies trained on seed $11$ and evaluated on the disjoint seed-$788$ set ($11+777$), and the LSM-D comparator refit and revalued out-of-sample at each bumped spot. The present-value levels reported below are therefore consistent with the corresponding entries of Table~\ref{tab:results} up to this smaller Monte Carlo sample.

\paragraph{Greeks via common-random-number revaluation.}
Delta and Gamma are obtained by revaluing the frozen policy on path bundles started at bumped initial spots $\{S_0(1-h),S_0,S_0(1+h)\}$ that share one simulation seed. Because the HHK draws are keyed off the seed and enter the spot only through the initial condition, the bump is the near-deterministic multiplicative shift $S_t(S_0')=S_t(S_0)\exp(\Delta\log S_0\,e^{-\alpha t})$ and the finite-difference noise cancels path by path. We use central differences with a relative bump $h=0.01$; the LSM-D value is refit and priced out-of-sample at each bumped spot. Figure~\ref{fig:hedging_greeks} reports the resulting price, Delta, and Gamma over a nine-point grid spanning $[0.5,2.0]\cdot S_0$. The RL-kernel value function reproduces the LSM-D benchmark to within $\pm 0.03$ price units across the whole grid ($\lesssim 0.5\%$ in relative terms for $S_0\gtrsim K$, with larger relative gaps appearing only at the lowest spots where the option value itself approaches zero), with coincident Delta and Gamma profiles. The model-agnostic RL policy prices systematically below both, by about $2.2\%$ near the money and more at the lowest spots, yet its Greeks stay close to the common profile: the no-kernel deficit is an exercise-quality effect on the \emph{level} of value, not a different risk profile.

\paragraph{Daily-rebalanced delta hedge.}
At each decision date $t$ the per-path continuation Delta $\Delta_t=\partial V_t/\partial S_t$ is computed by bumping the prompt spot and re-rolling the frozen policy to maturity; the bump propagates through the OU factor in closed form, $X_k\mapsto X_k+\delta e^{-\alpha(t_k-t)}$, so no resimulation is needed. The finite-difference Delta is pathwise before projection and therefore anticipates future shocks, so it is projected onto the date-$t$ state by a Longstaff--Schwartz regression~\cite{longstaff2001valuing} to make the hedge ratio $\mathcal F_t$-measurable. The position is hedged with the HHK forward to maturity, giving the discounted per-contract P\&L increment $\mathrm{DF}_{t+1}(F_{t+1}-F_t)$. The hedged seller P\&L has mean $\approx 0$ for both learned policies---the forward is a fair martingale and the hedge is non-anticipating---while the P\&L standard deviation falls by $\approx 51\%$ (from $\approx 2.50$ to $\approx 1.22$ for both variants), corresponding to roughly $76\%$ variance reduction. Hedge quality is essentially identical across the model-agnostic and kernel-augmented policies: the kernel's contribution is pricing accuracy, not hedgeability.

\paragraph{Hedged P\&L: Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall.}
To compare all three pricers on an equal footing, we additionally hedge each with the same regression (Longstaff--Schwartz) delta, an $\mathcal F_t$-measurable estimator obtained by regressing the realised continuation value on the date-$t$ state and differentiating in spot; this runs identically on RL and LSM-D cashflows~\cite{kolm2019dynamic,cao2022gamma}. Figure~\ref{fig:hedging_pnl} shows the terminal seller P\&L distributions with $95\%$ Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall (ES). Under the common regression-delta hedge, all three pricers reduce P\&L standard deviation by $39$--$42\%$ with nearly identical VaR and ES (VaR$_{95}\approx-2.4$, $\mathrm{ES}_{95}\approx-4.2$ to $-4.3$ across LSM-D, RL and RL-kernel), so hedge effectiveness does not separate them. The policy-specific continuation-bump hedge, available only for the differentiable RL policies, does better still ($\approx 51\%$ standard-deviation reduction and the tightest left tail, $\mathrm{ES}_{95}\approx -3.7$ versus $-4.2$/$-4.3$ for the regression hedge). In short, the kernel buys value-function accuracy, while the differentiable policy provides a direct continuation-Delta estimator that the capacity-constrained LSM dynamic program does not provide; neither advantage shows up as a difference in basic hedge effectiveness.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have formulated electricity swing-option valuation under the HHK jump-diffusion model as a finite-horizon stochastic control problem and approximated its solution with deterministic actor--critic methods. The central modelling device is a profitability-gated actor that clips candidate exercises to quantities with non-negative immediate payoff while preserving a usable gradient signal through a straight-through estimator.

\chg{conclusion para rewritten: DP now certifies absolute distance; $0.9$--$3.1\%$ / $0.3\%$ framing}%
\hlblock{On the HHK benchmark, the discretized-action LSM-D baseline is a strong classical comparator, and a near-exact grid-DP reference (Section~\ref{sec:dp_reference})---feasible because the testbed is two-factor with a tractable transition law---certifies every method's absolute distance from the optimum. The model-agnostic AC-sample trails the optimum by $0.9$--$3.1\%$, so for AC-sample the conclusion is competitiveness rather than dominance. AC-kernel is certified to lie within $0.3\%$ of the true value in every cell of the cost grid; it matches LSM-D under linear costs (where the coarse action grid is lossless) and exceeds the reported $M=5$ grid under convex costs (by up to $+5.1\%$), a gap the DP attributes to discretisation error in the baseline, whose certified shortfall reaches $-5.1\%$ in the convex corner while a finer action grid ($M=17$) recovers most of it. We therefore read the convex-cost evidence as certified near-optimality of both routes---a continuous-action method without an action grid, and a classical dynamic program given a sufficiently fine one---rather than as valuation dominance of either. AC-kernel's durable advantages over AC-sample are its far more reproducible benchmark gap across seeds (paired confidence intervals roughly an order of magnitude tighter) and grid-free continuous exercise. The present experiments focus on optional exercise with a cumulative cap and no active refraction lag.}

\chg{future-work item 1 reframed: DP already certifies distance here; dual bounds are for simulator-only models}%
\hlblock{The more durable takeaway is methodological: AC-sample provides a model-agnostic continuous-control route that relies only on simulated transitions, while AC-kernel shows how a known transition law can improve the critic target---in both accuracy and seed stability---without changing the policy architecture. The same policy family also delivers usable risk output: common-random-number finite-difference Greeks that track the benchmark and daily-rebalanced delta hedges with similar hedge quality across methods (Section~\ref{sec:hedging}). Several directions for future work follow naturally. First, on the present testbed the absolute distance to the optimum is already certified by the grid-DP reference of Section~\ref{sec:dp_reference}; for models that offer only a simulator---where that reference is unavailable---constructing dual upper bounds, for example via an adaptation of the Andersen--Broadie or Haugh--Kogan methodology~\cite{andersen2004primal,haugh2004pricing} to the swing setting, remains the natural certification route. Second, the regime where continuous control is expected to pay off is one the present testbed deliberately excludes: contracts with mandatory take obligations, non-trivial refraction lags, or ramping constraints, where the discrete-grid dynamic program must augment its state and the grid-free continuous policy may have a structural edge. Third, calibration to observed forward and option data would broaden the practical relevance of both approaches.}

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{appendix:reproducibility}

All reported results are regenerated from archived artifacts by a deterministic pipeline; the elements needed to reproduce them are collected here.

\paragraph{Seeds and data.} Each actor--critic cell is trained with eight seeds $\{11,\dots,18\}$ for $32{,}768$ episodes. Valuation prices each training seed $k$ on its own out-of-sample set of $N_{\mathrm{test}}=65{,}536$ HHK paths drawn with the dedicated evaluation seed $k+777$, so the reported per-cell statistics span both initialisation and Monte~Carlo variation; on each such set the LSM-D benchmark and both actor--critic variants are priced on the \emph{same} paths, making $\Delta\%$ a seed-paired quantity. The LSM-D continuation regressions are fit on an independent sample of $32{,}768$ paths. Path generation uses scrambled Sobol draws for the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck increments. Training, fitting, and evaluation samples are disjoint.

\paragraph{Time grid.} The contract uses $n=22$ decision dates spanning $n-1=21$ intervals with $\Delta t = T/(n-1)$ (Section~\ref{sec:market_contract}); the time-to-maturity feature is the affine surrogate $(n-i)/(n-1)$.

\paragraph{Checkpoints and artifacts.} Reported weights are the evaluation EMA of the actor weights for both variants; no best-validation selection is used. Each table entry is reproducible from a per-run configuration (\texttt{runs/<name>.json}) and weights (\texttt{runs/<name>.pth}), the aggregated results file, and the pivot command that produces Table~\ref{tab:results}; these are collected in the artifact manifest. Hyperparameters are listed in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}.

\paragraph{Feature preprocessing.} The spot component enters as log-moneyness $\log(S_{t_i}/K)$; the latent factors $X_{t_i},Y_{t_i}$ pass through a robust-normalisation layer that, in the reported runs, uses default identity statistics (median $0$, IQR $1$), so they enter essentially unscaled apart from the hidden-layer normalisation.

\paragraph{LSM-D benchmark.} The benchmark uses $M=5$ exercise levels and the full HHK market state, with inventory and cooldown carried as discrete slices; continuation values are fit with a degree-$2$ total-degree Chebyshev basis (no regularisation) and per-date features min--max scaled to $[-1,1]$. The basis and action grid are fixed a priori, so no validation-based model selection is performed. The action-grid sensitivity of Table~\ref{tab:lsm_grid} refits LSM-D at $M\in\{5,9,17\}$ on the same per-seed out-of-sample sets (script \texttt{gen\_table6\_v67.py}, output \texttt{table6\_lsm\_grid\_v67.csv}).

\chg{new appendix paragraph documenting the DP solver + sweep scripts}\hlpar{DP reference.}{The grid-DP solver (\texttt{grid\_dp\_pricer/}, FP64 C++) is validated by a unit suite (kernel parity against an independent implementation of the analytical HHK kernel, closed-form one-step moments, spline and quadrature exactness, limiting cases) plus a twelve-test verification notebook. The per-cell reference values and their numerical-uncertainty bands are produced by \texttt{tools/dp\_publication\_sweep.py} (output \texttt{results/dp\_publication\_sweep.csv}, which records for every cell the full grid configuration, truncation ranges $X\in[-1.6,1.6]$, $Y\in[0,4]$, refinement-ladder values, observed order, and band): convex cells use a geometric $n_Q\in\{101,201,401\}$ ladder at fixed $121^2$ spatial nodes and $M_x=24$ Gauss--Hermite nodes with three-point Richardson extrapolation and a Grid Convergence Index band (factor of safety $1.25$); linear-payoff cells ($\gamma_c=1$ or $c=0$) are solved in the exact-lattice mode ($n_Q=11$, node-aligned bang-bang control) with the band taken as $1.25\times$ the oscillation envelope of the four finest members of a spatial ladder $n_X=n_Y\in\{281,\dots,641\}$ at $M_x=48$. Forward-simulation self-consistency checks are recorded in \texttt{results/dp\_forward\_mc\_checks.csv}. The body of Table~\ref{tab:results} (DP column included) is regenerated by \texttt{tools/gen\_table5\_dp.py}; Figure~\ref{fig:dp_convergence} is produced by \texttt{grid\_dp\_pricer/tools/gen\_paper\_figs.py}.}

\paragraph{AC-kernel quadrature.} The expected critic target uses a shared Gauss--Hermite rule with $M_x=2$ nodes for the Gaussian $X$ transition and a single-mesh quadrature ($M_{\mathrm{per}\,k}=1$, $N_{\max}=1$) for the rapidly decaying spike factor $Y$ (Section~\ref{sec:actor_critic}).

\paragraph{Expected-target ablation.} The $+2.5$ percentage-point figure quoted in the discussion is the focal matched ablation: the AC-kernel recipe run with the expected target on versus off (the only change), at $c=0.04$, $\gamma_c=2$, $4{,}096$ episodes, $12$ seeds $\{11,\dots,22\}$, evaluated on the common test set. It is focal evidence, not a grid-wide claim. The comparison and its per-seed values are recorded in the project hyperparameter-tuning log (\texttt{HPT.md}) and the Validation-3 semi-analytical-kernel study notebook.

\paragraph{Risk study.} The Greeks and hedges of Section~\ref{sec:hedging} use the seed-$11$ focal checkpoints with common-random-number central differences (relative bump $h=0.01$), evaluated out-of-sample on $8{,}192$ valuation and $4{,}096$ hedging paths (the seed-$11$ policy priced on the disjoint seed-$788$ set, $11+777$); the LSM-D comparator is refit and revalued at each bumped spot. The focal risk-study present values (RL-kernel $1.985$, AC-sample $1.958$, LSM-D $1.972$) match the corresponding Table~\ref{tab:results} entries up to this smaller Monte Carlo sample.
